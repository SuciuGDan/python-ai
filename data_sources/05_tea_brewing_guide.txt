A Beginner's Guide to Brewing Tea

Where tea comes from

All true tea comes from a single plant, Camellia sinensis. White, green, oolong, black and pu-erh tea are all made from its leaves. The difference between them is how the leaves are processed after picking. Green tea is heated quickly to stop oxidation, so it stays fresh and grassy. Black tea is fully oxidised, which turns the leaves dark and gives a stronger, maltier taste. Oolong sits between the two, with partial oxidation. Herbal infusions such as chamomile or peppermint contain no leaves from the tea plant, so strictly speaking they are not tea at all.

According to a Chinese legend, tea was discovered by the emperor Shennong in about 2737 BC, when a few leaves blew into his pot of boiling water. Tea reached Europe in the early 1600s through Dutch traders, and it became a fashionable drink in England during the following century.

Water temperature and steeping time

The same leaves can taste bitter or pleasant depending on the water. As a general guide, white tea needs water at 75 to 85 degrees Celsius and about 2 to 3 minutes. Green tea likes water at 75 to 80 degrees and 2 to 3 minutes. Oolong is best at 85 to 95 degrees for 3 to 5 minutes. Black tea can take water close to boiling, at 95 to 100 degrees, for 3 to 5 minutes. Water that is too hot burns delicate green leaves and makes them bitter, which is the most common beginner mistake.

Use about one teaspoon of loose leaves for every 250 millilitres of water, and always give the leaves room to open. A tea bag squeezed into a tiny cup cannot release its flavour properly. Fresh, cold water gives better results than water that has been boiled several times.

The Copper Kettle tea house

In the small town of Millbrook, the Copper Kettle tea house was opened in 1998 by Dara Quill, a former librarian who had spent two years travelling through Asia. The shop has only eight tables, a long wooden counter and a wall of glass jars filled with loose leaves. Dara keeps a rule that no tea is ever served in a paper cup.

The most popular drink at the Copper Kettle is a house blend called Evening Ember. It combines smoky black tea, dried orange peel and a pinch of cinnamon, and it is brewed for four minutes at 98 degrees. The quietest day of the week is Wednesday, when Dara teaches a free class for beginners. Students learn to taste three teas side by side and write down what they notice, from the smell of the dry leaves to the colour of the cup.

Storing your tea

Tea absorbs smells easily, so keep it in an airtight tin away from light, heat and strong spices. Green and white teas are best used within a year, while black tea and pu-erh keep their quality for much longer. Never store tea in the fridge, because moisture will spoil it.
